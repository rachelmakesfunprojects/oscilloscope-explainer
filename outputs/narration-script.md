## Scene 1 — Overview (estimated 00:00–00:22)

Meet the world's tiniest pencil. No lead. Just electrons. A classic analog oscilloscope turns a changing voltage into a line of light. Inside its glass tube is an electron gun: a little assembly that releases, controls, accelerates and focuses electrons. Let us follow them.

## Scene 2 — Cathode and heater (estimated 00:22–01:06)

First, the heater. In this common design, a small filament warms a separate, coated cathode. This is an indirectly heated cathode: the heater supplies warmth; the cathode supplies the beam's electrons. Heat gives some electrons enough energy to escape the surface, a process called thermionic emission. They were already in the material; we have not invented new electrons. Electrons arriving through the cathode's electrical connection replace those carried away by the beam. Meanwhile, the tube's vacuum removes nearly all the gas, allowing electrons to travel with very few collisions.

## Scene 3 — Control grid (estimated 01:06–01:43)

Immediately ahead sits the control grid, usually a small metal cup with an opening. It is held negative relative to the cathode. Its electric field restricts how many electrons can enter the beam. Make it less negative, and more pass through: a brighter trace. Make it more negative, and the beam dwindles. At cutoff, it is stopped. Nothing has to slide shut. This electrical gate can blank the beam between sweeps. A remarkably punctual doorman.

## Scene 4 — Acceleration (estimated 01:43–02:12)

Next, accelerating electrodes, called anodes, establish voltages more positive than the cathode. Their electric fields give the electrons forward speed as they pass through aligned openings. 'Positive' here means relative to the cathode, not necessarily positive relative to ground. The power supply provides this energy. The heater releases electrons from the surface; these voltage differences do the accelerating.

## Scene 5 — Focusing (estimated 02:12–02:44)

Speed alone would give us a rather untidy splodge. Shaped electrodes at carefully chosen voltages create an electrostatic lens. Its curved electric field bends the paths of electrons towards a small spot on the screen, much as an optical lens brings light to a focus. There is no piece of glass in this lens. Turn the focus control, and you adjust its electrical strength.

## Scene 6 — Deflection (estimated 02:44–03:11)

Now the beam reaches two pairs of deflection plates, arranged at right angles. Because electrons carry negative charge, they bend towards the more positive plate. One pair steers vertically; the other horizontally. The input signal, suitably amplified, drives the vertical pair. As that voltage rises and falls, the spot follows it up and down.

## Scene 7 — Timebase and phosphor (estimated 03:11–03:43)

To spread that movement across time, a ramp voltage sweeps the spot steadily from left to right. During the rapid return, called flyback, the beam is blanked. At the screen, energetic electrons excite a phosphor coating, which emits light and briefly continues glowing. The travelling spot and its fading trail make the waveform. Our drawn beam path is an illustration; the light appears at the screen.

## Scene 8 — Trigger (estimated 03:43–04:10)

Finally, the trigger waits for a chosen event, such as the signal crossing a selected voltage in the rising direction. Each sweep then begins at the same point in a repeating signal, so the trace settles. Height tells us voltage; distance across tells us time. An electron gun, some careful steering, and rather lovely handwriting.

## Sources and production accuracy notes

These are factual references, kept separate from the spoken script. All narration is original; no source text is quoted.

- **Cathode and heater:** [Tektronix, Cathode Ray Tubes: Getting Down to Basics (1989), printed pages 4–5](https://usermanual.wiki/m/fc136b16b15b162b65d10abdf983e39384b92f958ff66780e1174d1131677057.pdf) describes a separate heater inserted inside the coated cathode cylinder and the thermal energy of emitted electrons. This supports the representative indirectly heated construction shown. Heat supplies energy to existing electrons. Replenishment through the external connection follows ordinary current continuity; it is not an electron-creation process.
- **Acceleration and focusing:** [Tektronix, Chuck DeVere, Cathode-Ray Tubes, second edition (1969)](https://w140.com/tekwiki/images/6/62/062-0852-01.pdf), printed pages 3–13 and 25–26, explains electrode potentials, electric-field shape and electron lenses. Show anodes as more positive relative to the cathode; avoid implying every electrode is above ground. Keep focusing and acceleration understandable as related electric-field effects, even though separate scenes teach them.
- **Grid control and blanking:** [Tektronix, Cathode Ray Tubes: Getting Down to Basics (1989)](https://usermanual.wiki/m/fc136b16b15b162b65d10abdf983e39384b92f958ff66780e1174d1131677057.pdf), printed pages 5–6 and 10–11, covers grid bias, cutoff and grid unblanking. The script chooses grid blanking for this representative design. The grid changes an electrical barrier; its aperture does not physically close. Cathode emission need not cease when the beam is cut off.
- **Vacuum and electrostatic steering:** [Tektronix, Harold Magee, Oscilloscope Trigger Circuits (1969), introduction, printed page 1](https://www.bitsavers.org/pdf/tektronix/concepts/062-1056-00_Oscilloscope_Trigger_Circuits_Feb69.pdf) describes the evacuated envelope, electron optics and X/Y electric deflection. Draw orthogonal plate pairs and deflection towards the more positive plate. The tube's instantaneous beam is not a sinusoidal path.
- **Signal, sweep and trigger:** [Tektronix 2215 service manual, Theory of Operation](https://download.tek.com/manual/tek_2215_service.pdf) describes vertical amplification, linear sweep and triggered operation. Use a rising-edge threshold crossing as the simple trigger example. The “height = voltage; across = time” explanation assumes ordinary swept operation, not X–Y mode.
- **Light and persistence:** [Tektronix, Fundamentals of Real-Time Spectrum Analysis, printed page 26](https://download.tek.com/document/37W_17249_4.pdf) explains phosphor excitation, fading afterglow and intensity. The visible beam inside the cutaway is an explanatory overlay; the screen provides the visible spot. Slow down the animation and exaggerate the trail as needed to make the mechanism legible.
